The science of the night

Why Titanic Floated, and Why She Sank

A ship weighing about 52,000 tonnes, built mostly of steel, should sink like a stone. Titanic floated for one reason, discovered more than 2,000 years earlier by a Greek mathematician.

Archimedes’ principle

Archimedes lived in Syracuse, Sicily, around 250 BC. According to legend, he worked out the idea while stepping into a bath and watching the water rise, then ran through the streets shouting “Eureka!”, meaning “I have found it!”

Any object in water is pushed upward by a force equal to the weight of the water it pushes aside.
Buoyant force = density of water × volume pushed aside × g

When you lower something into water, it shoves water out of the way. That displaced water wants its place back, so it pushes up on the object. If the push is as big as the object’s weight before it’s fully underwater, the object floats. If not, it sinks.

How a steel ship floats

Steel is about 7.7 times denser than seawater, so a lump of it sinks. But Titanic was not a lump. Her hull was a giant steel shell full of air: cabins, corridors, boiler rooms and cargo holds. Averaged over that whole volume, the ship was much lighter than the same volume of seawater.

So she settled into the sea until the water she pushed aside weighed exactly as much as she did. That happened when her hull was about 10.5 metres deep in the water.

Titanic’s weight, fully loadedabout 52,000 tonnes
Density of seawaterabout 1,025 kg/m³
Seawater she had to push aside to float (52,000 t ÷ 1.025 t/m³)about 50,700 m³
That’s roughly the volume of20 Olympic pools
Depth of hull below the waterline (draught)about 10.5 m

Why she sank anyway

Titanic’s hull was divided into 16 watertight compartments by 15 steel walls called bulkheads. If a few flooded, the rest would still push aside enough water to keep her up. She was designed to stay afloat with any two compartments flooded, or with the first four at the bow.

The iceberg opened six. As seawater poured in, it replaced air, so the ship grew heavier without getting any bigger. To push aside enough water to balance that extra weight, she had to sink deeper, and the bow went down first.

The bulkheads didn’t reach all the way to the top deck. As the bow dropped, water rose over the top of one bulkhead and spilled into the next compartment, then the next, like water filling an ice-cube tray tipped at one end. Once that started, sinking couldn’t be stopped.

This model is simplified to show the idea. The real ship sank over 2 hours and 40 minutes.

The lesson

Archimedes’ principle never stopped working that night. Right up to the end, the sea pushed up on Titanic with a force exactly equal to the weight of the water she displaced. What changed was what was inside her. Every tonne of seawater that replaced air made the ship heavier, until no amount of sinking deeper could push aside enough water to hold her up.
