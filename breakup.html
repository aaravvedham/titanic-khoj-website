Why She Broke Apart

For over 70 years people argued about whether Titanic sank in one piece. The wreck, found in 1985, settled it: she broke in two. Watch it happen, then see why.

Water only holds up what is in the water

Archimedes’ principle works only on the parts of a ship that are under the surface. As the flooded bow sank, the stern rose higher and higher into the air, and the part above the water got no upward push from the sea at all.

Like a heavy plank over the edge of a table

The raised stern weighed thousands of tonnes and hung out over the water like a heavy plank sticking out over the edge of a table. The further it stuck out, the stronger its bending effect, because turning effect = force × distance.

That bending was strongest near the waterline, roughly between the third and fourth funnels. Steel can only take so much, and at about 2:18 am the hull there tore apart. The bow broke away and sank. The stern briefly settled back, filled with water, rose until it was almost vertical, and slipped under at 2:20 am.

What the wreck tells us

Robert Ballard’s team found the wreck in 1985, about 3,800 m down, in two main pieces lying around 600 m apart. Many experts now think she broke while the stern was raised only about 15–25°, far less steep than the 45° shown in many old paintings.
